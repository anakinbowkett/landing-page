Simple and Compound Interest

Calculate interest earned on savings or charged on loans using both the simple and compound methods.

What This Is

Interest is the extra money you earn when you save, or the extra money you owe when you borrow. Simple interest pays the same fixed amount every year. Compound interest is more powerful — each year the interest is added to the total, so next year you earn interest on a bigger amount. Edexcel exam questions almost always ask for compound interest, so that is the main method here.

Key Terms You Must Know

Principal (P)

The original amount of money you start with — before any interest is added. Also called the initial investment or starting amount.

Rate (r)

The percentage of interest applied each year. A rate of 3% means you gain 3p for every £1 each year. Always convert a percentage to a decimal before using it in a formula: 3% → 0.03.

Simple interest

Interest calculated on the original principal only. The same fixed amount is added every year. Formula: SI = P × r × t.

Compound interest

Interest calculated on the growing total each year. Each year's interest is added to the balance, so the next year's interest is larger. Formula: A = P × (1 + r)ⁿ.

Multiplier

The number you multiply by to apply one year of compound interest. For a rate of 3%, the multiplier is 1.03. For a decrease (depreciation), the multiplier is less than 1.

The Method — Step by Step
Grade 5–7 topic. Simple interest appears from Grade 5; compound interest using the multiplier formula is Grade 6–7 on Edexcel.
1

Simple interest — multiply the principal by the rate and the time

Write the simple interest formula: SI = P × r × t. P is the starting amount, r is the rate as a decimal, and t is the number of years. Simple interest pays the same fixed amount every single year — the interest never grows because it is always calculated on the original P, not on the running total.

Convert the percentage rate to a decimal by dividing by 100, then substitute P, r and t into the formula. The result is the total interest earned — not the final balance. Add SI to P if the question asks for the total amount at the end.

Example: £2000 at 3% for 4 years. SI = 2000 × 0.03 × 4 = £240. Total = 2000 + 240 = £2240.
2

Understand why compound interest grows faster — interest earns interest

With simple interest, each year adds the same fixed £60 to £2000 — the interest is always 3% of the original £2000. With compound interest, Year 1 adds £60 to give £2060, but Year 2 adds 3% of £2060 (£61.80), not 3% of £2000. Each year starts from a larger base, so each year's interest is larger than the last.

Track the compound growth year by year: Year 1: £2000 → £2060. Year 2: £2060 → £2121.80. Year 3: £2121.80 → £2185.45. Year 4: £2185.45 → £2251.02. Every year the increase is slightly larger because the base amount is larger. This is the core idea of compounding.

Notice: the interest added rises each year — £60.00, £61.80, £63.65, £65.56 — because 3% is always applied to the growing total, not the original £2000.
3

Build the multiplier — write (1 + r) using the rate as a decimal

Convert the percentage rate to a decimal, then add 1. The reason you add 1 is that you are keeping the original amount (the "1") and gaining the interest (the decimal part) on top. For a rate of 3%: convert to 0.03, then add 1 to get the multiplier 1.03.

Write the full compound interest formula: A = P × (1 + r)ⁿ. A is the final amount, P is the starting amount, (1 + r) is the multiplier, and n is the number of years. The power n means you apply the multiplier n times — once for each year.

Example: 3% interest for 4 years. Multiplier = 1 + 0.03 = 1.03. Formula becomes: A = 2000 × 1.03⁴.
4

Apply the formula — substitute values and evaluate the power

Substitute P, the multiplier, and n into the formula, writing each value clearly. Write A = 2000 × 1.03⁴ as the working line. On your calculator type: 2000 × 1.03 ^ 4 and press equals. Do not round the multiplier before pressing equals — keep the full decimal to avoid rounding errors.

Round the final answer to the nearest penny — two decimal places — unless the question specifies otherwise. Write the answer with a £ sign and two decimal places. The mark scheme requires the correctly rounded value, so always write the full answer before rounding.

Example: A = 2000 × 1.03⁴ = 2000 × 1.12550881 = 2251.018... Round to £2251.02.
5

In the event of a decrease or depreciation — use a multiplier less than 1

When a question asks for a value that decreases by a percentage each year — a car losing value, a population falling — use exactly the same formula, but subtract the rate from 1 instead of adding it. For a 10% decrease per year: multiplier = 1 − 0.10 = 0.90.

Apply exactly the same A = P × multiplierⁿ formula. If a car worth £12 000 depreciates by 15% per year for 3 years: multiplier = 1 − 0.15 = 0.85, so A = 12 000 × 0.85³. Calculate 0.85³ = 0.614125, then A = 12 000 × 0.614125 = £7369.50.

Depreciation formula: A = P × (1 − r)ⁿ. Same structure as compound interest — only the multiplier changes from >1 to <1.
