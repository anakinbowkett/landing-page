Midpoint and Distance

Find the exact middle of a line segment and calculate its length using two coordinate points.

What This Is

Given two points on a grid, you can find two things: the midpoint (the exact halfway point between them) and the distance (how long the straight line between them is). The midpoint formula averages the x-coordinates and the y-coordinates separately. The distance formula uses Pythagoras — the straight-line distance is the hypotenuse of a hidden right-angled triangle. Both formulas are straightforward once you see where they come from.

Key Terms You Must Know

Midpoint

The point exactly halfway along a line segment between two given points. It sits at the average of the x-coordinates and the average of the y-coordinates: M = ((x₁+x₂)/2, (y₁+y₂)/2).

Distance formula

The length of the straight line between two points, derived from Pythagoras: d = √((x₂−x₁)² + (y₂−y₁)²). The horizontal and vertical gaps form the two shorter sides of a right-angled triangle; the distance is the hypotenuse.

Coordinates

A pair of numbers (x, y) that give a point's exact location on a grid. The first number is how far right (x), the second is how far up (y).

Surd

A square root that cannot be simplified to a whole number, left in the form √n or a√b. Exam questions often ask for distance as a surd — leave it as 2√13, not as a decimal.

Hypotenuse

The longest side of a right-angled triangle, always opposite the right angle. In the distance formula, the straight-line distance AB is the hypotenuse of the triangle formed by the horizontal and vertical gaps.

The Method — Step by Step
Grade 5–7 topic. Midpoint appears from Grade 5; the distance formula and surd simplification are Grade 6–7 on Edexcel.
1

Label the two points — write each x and y value clearly before using any formula

Read the two coordinates from the question and write them out with labels. Call the first point (x₁, y₁) and the second point (x₂, y₂). This step prevents the most common error — substituting the wrong number into the wrong slot of the formula.

Plot both points on a quick sketch grid. Seeing the two points on a grid immediately shows you roughly where the midpoint should land and how far apart the points are — which gives you a way to sense-check your answers at the end.

Example: A(1, 2) and B(7, 6). Label: x₁=1, y₁=2, x₂=7, y₂=6.
2

Find the midpoint — average the x-values, then average the y-values

Write the midpoint formula: M = ((x₁+x₂)/2, (y₁+y₂)/2). The midpoint is simply the mean of the two x-coordinates and the mean of the two y-coordinates — you are finding the halfway point in each direction separately.

Substitute x₁=1, x₂=7 into the x part: (1+7)/2 = 8/2 = 4. Then substitute y₁=2, y₂=6 into the y part: (2+6)/2 = 8/2 = 4. Work out each fraction one at a time — do not rush both together.

Write the midpoint as a coordinate pair in brackets: M = (4, 4). Check it makes sense — the midpoint (4, 4) should sit visually between A(1, 2) and B(7, 6) when you look at your sketch.

Example: A(1, 2) and B(7, 6). Midpoint = ((1+7)/2, (2+6)/2) = (4, 4).
3

Find the distance — draw the hidden right-angled triangle, then use Pythagoras

Draw a right-angled triangle by adding a third point C that is directly across from A and directly below B. C sits at (x₂, y₁) — it shares its x-coordinate with B and its y-coordinate with A. For A(1, 2) and B(7, 6): C is at (7, 2).

Count the two shorter sides: AC is the horizontal gap (x₂ − x₁ = 7 − 1 = 6) and CB is the vertical gap (y₂ − y₁ = 6 − 2 = 4). Label these on the triangle. These are the two legs of the right-angled triangle.

Apply Pythagoras to find AB: d² = 6² + 4² = 36 + 16 = 52. Take the square root: d = √52. Simplify if needed — √52 = √(4 × 13) = 2√13. Always leave as a surd unless the question asks for a decimal.

Example: A(1, 2) and B(7, 6). d = √((7−1)² + (6−2)²) = √(36 + 16) = √52 = 2√13.
4

Simplify the surd — find the largest square factor and take its root outside

Look at the number under the square root sign (here, 52). Find the largest square number that divides into it. The square numbers are 4, 9, 16, 25, 36 … For 52: try 4 — 52 ÷ 4 = 13, so 52 = 4 × 13, and 4 is a perfect square.

Write √52 = √(4 × 13) = √4 × √13 = 2√13. The 2 comes out in front of the root because √4 = 2. The √13 stays inside because 13 has no square factors. The final answer for the distance is 2√13.

Example: √52 = √(4 × 13) = 2√13. This is the exact distance from A(1, 2) to B(7, 6).
5

In the event of a missing endpoint — use the midpoint formula in reverse

Some questions give you one endpoint and the midpoint, then ask for the other endpoint. Set up two separate equations — one for x, one for y — using the midpoint formula. For example: midpoint M(4, 4), one endpoint A(1, 2). Let the missing endpoint be B(p, q).

Multiply both sides of each equation by 2, then solve. For x: 1 + p = 8, so p = 7. For y: 2 + q = 8, so q = 6. The missing endpoint is B(7, 6) — which matches our original point, confirming the method works correctly.

Example: M(4, 4), A(1, 2), find B. x: (1+p)/2 = 4 → p = 7. y: (2+q)/2 = 4 → q = 6. B = (7, 6).
