Probability — Rules (AND / OR)

How to find the probability of combined events using the addition rule and the multiplication rule

What This Is

When a question asks about two events happening together, you need a rule for combining their probabilities. The OR rule finds the probability that at least one of two events occurs — you add their probabilities, then subtract any overlap to avoid counting it twice. The AND rule finds the probability that both events occur — for independent events, you multiply their probabilities together. Choosing the right rule depends on whether the question asks for "at least one of" or "both of".

Key Terms You Must Know

Mutually Exclusive Events

Two events that cannot happen at the same time. If a card is even it cannot simultaneously be odd. For mutually exclusive events, P(A and B) = 0, so the OR rule simplifies to P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B).

Independent Events

Two events where the outcome of one does not affect the probability of the other. Drawing a card, replacing it, then drawing again produces independent events. The AND rule for independent events is P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B).

The OR Rule (Addition Rule)

P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A and B). The subtraction removes the overlap — any outcomes that satisfy both A and B would otherwise be counted twice when you add P(A) and P(B).

The AND Rule (Multiplication Rule)

P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B), but only when A and B are independent. If the events are not independent — for example, drawing without replacement — the conditional probability P(B|A) is used instead of P(B).

Venn Diagram

A rectangle (representing all possible outcomes) containing two overlapping circles (representing the two events). Outcomes in the overlap satisfy both events simultaneously. Outcomes outside both circles satisfy neither.

The Method — Step by Step
Grade 5–7 topic. The OR and AND rules appear on every Edexcel paper. The overlap subtraction in the OR rule is the most commonly missed step.
1

List the outcomes of each event and find the probability of each

Read the question and write out every outcome that satisfies event A, then every outcome that satisfies event B. Listing them forces you to see exactly what is in each event — and whether any outcomes appear in both. Count the outcomes in each list and write P(A) and P(B) as fractions over the total number of equally likely outcomes.

Cards 1–10. A = even: {2, 4, 6, 8, 10} → P(A) = 5/10. B = greater than 7: {8, 9, 10} → P(B) = 3/10.
2

Find the overlap — the outcomes that satisfy both events at once

Compare your two lists and circle any outcome that appears in both. These outcomes are in the overlap — they satisfy event A and event B simultaneously. Count them, write them as a set, and express them as a probability over the total. Draw a Venn diagram to see the overlap clearly: the two circles represent the two events, and the region where they cross represents the outcomes that are in both.

Write P(A and B) as a fraction. The outcomes in both A and B are {8, 10} — two outcomes out of ten. So P(A and B) = 2/10. This value is what you subtract in the OR rule to prevent double-counting those two outcomes.

A and B = {8, 10} — both even and greater than 7. P(A and B) = 2/10.
3

Apply the OR rule: P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A and B)

Write the formula on its own line first — P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A and B) — then substitute the three values you have found. Adding P(A) and P(B) counts the overlap twice because those outcomes appear in both circles. Subtracting P(A and B) once brings the count back to the correct total.

Combine the fractions over the common denominator and simplify. Since all three fractions share a denominator of 10, you add and subtract the numerators: 5 + 3 − 2 = 6. Write the result as 6/10, then cancel down by dividing numerator and denominator by 2 to reach the simplest form.

P(A or B) = 5/10 + 3/10 − 2/10 = 6/10 = 3/5.
4

The AND rule — multiply the probabilities of independent events

Identify that the question is asking for both events to happen — "A and B" or "both". For independent events (where the first outcome does not affect the second), write the AND rule: P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B). Draw a probability tree with two branches at the first level and two at the second — the path along both desired branches gives you the calculation.

Multiply P(A) by P(B) to find the probability that both happen. Write the multiplication as a product of two fractions: numerator × numerator over denominator × denominator. Then simplify if possible. Each fraction on the tree path gets multiplied — the product gives the probability of following that specific sequence of branches.

P(A) × P(B) = 5/10 × 3/10 = 15/100 = 3/20. Divide both by 5 to simplify.
5

In the event of mutually exclusive events — drop the subtraction entirely

Identify whether the two events can both happen at the same time. If they cannot — for example, a card cannot be both red and black, a die cannot show both 3 and 5 — the events are mutually exclusive and P(A and B) = 0. The OR rule then simplifies to P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B), with no subtraction needed.

Write P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B) directly without the subtraction term. Edexcel questions on mutually exclusive events often give the probabilities of several outcomes summing to 1 and ask you to find a missing value — set up the equation P(A) + P(B) + P(C) + ... = 1 and solve.

If events A, B, C are mutually exclusive with P(A) = 0.3, P(C) = 0.5, then P(B) = 1 − 0.3 − 0.5 = 0.2.

Multiplication is the AND rule — this question asks for OR!

0 / 3 marks
Multiplication gives the probability that both events happen — that is the AND rule. "Or" means at least one of the two events occurs, which always requires addition (minus the overlap), never multiplication. A student who multiplies P(A) × P(B) for an "or" question gets a far smaller answer than the correct one, because the true probability of at least one event is always larger than the probability of both. Read the question carefully: "or" signals the addition rule; "and" signals the multiplication rule. They are not interchangeable.
