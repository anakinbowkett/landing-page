Trigonometry in 3D

Finding lengths and angles inside cuboids, pyramids and prisms by identifying flat triangles within the solid

What This Is

3D trigonometry is not a new type of trigonometry — it is the same SOH CAH TOA and Pythagoras you already know, applied inside a three-dimensional shape. The entire skill is this: find the right flat triangle hidden inside the 3D solid, draw it separately, label its sides, then solve it as a 2D problem. Every question, no matter how complex the shape looks, reduces to exactly this process.

Key Terms You Must Know

Space Diagonal

A diagonal that cuts through the interior of a 3D solid from one corner to the opposite corner — passing through the air inside, not along any face. In a cuboid ABCDA'B'C'D', the space diagonal runs from A to C' (or B to D', etc.).

Face Diagonal

A diagonal that lies entirely on one face of the solid. It is a flat 2D line on the surface — not through the interior. You often calculate face diagonals as a first step before finding the space diagonal or an angle.

Angle of Elevation

The angle measured upward from a horizontal base line to a sloping line. In 3D, this is usually the angle between a space diagonal and the horizontal base of the solid.

The Key Step — Extract the Triangle

Before writing any formula, identify the three points that form the right-angled triangle you need. Draw that triangle separately beside the 3D diagram. Label its sides using lengths from the shape. Then solve it in 2D.

Two-Stage Problems

Many 3D trig questions require two triangles: first use Pythagoras on a base face to find a face diagonal, then use that result as one side of a second triangle to find the space diagonal or angle. These are always solved in order — base first, then vertical.

The Method — Step by Step
Grade 7–9 topic. Trigonometry in 3D appears on both calculator and non-calculator papers. The method is identical every time — the only variable is which triangle you extract. Getting the triangle wrong is the only way to get the question wrong.
1

Understand the core idea: every 3D trig problem hides a flat right-angled triangle inside the solid

A 3D solid like a cuboid is built entirely from flat rectangular faces. Any diagonal you draw — whether across a face or through the interior — forms one side of a right-angled triangle whose other two sides lie along edges of the shape. Your job is to find that hidden triangle, pull it out of the 3D picture, and solve it in 2D. Everything else is just recognising which triangle to extract.

The right angle in the extracted triangle almost always sits where a vertical edge meets a horizontal base. In a cuboid, every vertical edge is perpendicular to the base — so any triangle formed by a base diagonal, a vertical edge, and the space diagonal through the corner will contain a perfect right angle at the base of the vertical edge. Find that right angle first, then label the other two sides.

To find the space diagonal of a cuboid: the right angle sits at the far corner of the base, between the face diagonal (horizontal) and the vertical edge of the cuboid. Extract that right-angled triangle and solve with Pythagoras.
2

Stage 1 of every cuboid problem — find the base face diagonal using Pythagoras

Almost every 3D trig question about a cuboid is a two-stage problem. Stage 1: look at the bottom face of the cuboid. It is a flat rectangle. Draw its diagonal — this is a face diagonal. The diagonal, the length, and the width of the base form a right-angled triangle entirely on the bottom face. Use Pythagoras to find this face diagonal. Write it down — you need it in Stage 2.

Keep the exact value of the face diagonal — do not round it. You will substitute it into Stage 2, and rounding here creates an error in the final answer. If d = √(l² + w²), leave it written as a square root until you need to use it. For a cuboid with length 8 and width 6: d = √(64 + 36) = √100 = 10. This is exact, so continue with 10.

Cuboid 8 × 6 × 5. Stage 1: face diagonal of base = √(8² + 6²) = √100 = 10. Write this down before Stage 2.
3

Stage 2 — use the face diagonal and the height to find the space diagonal or angle

Now build the second triangle. It stands upright inside the cuboid. Its base is the face diagonal you found in Stage 1. Its vertical side is the height of the cuboid. Its hypotenuse is the space diagonal — the line cutting through the air from one corner of the base to the opposite top corner. This is a right-angled triangle because the height of the cuboid is vertical and the base is horizontal.

Use Pythagoras or trigonometry on this second triangle depending on what the question asks. To find the space diagonal: use Pythagoras — space diagonal = √(face diagonal² + height²). To find the angle of elevation (θ) from one corner to the opposite top corner: use tan θ = height / face diagonal. Apply cos⁻¹, sin⁻¹ or tan⁻¹ as appropriate.

Cuboid 8×6×5. Stage 1: face diagonal = 10. Stage 2: space diagonal = √(10² + 5²) = √125 ≈ 11.18. Angle of elevation = tan⁻¹(5/10) = 26.6°.
4

Pyramids and prisms — identify the triangle from the apex to the base centre

For a pyramid, the key triangle has three points: the apex (top point), the midpoint of a base edge, and the centre of the base. The apex is directly above the centre of the base — so the edge from the centre to directly below the apex is vertical, and the line from the centre to the midpoint of a base edge is horizontal. These two lines meet at a right angle.

For a square-based pyramid with base length 2a and height h: the horizontal distance from the centre of the base to the midpoint of a base edge is a (half the base length). The triangle formed by the apex, centre, and midpoint of a base edge has a right angle at the centre. Its sides are h (vertical), a (horizontal), and the slant height l as the hypotenuse. Use tan(angle) = h/a to find the angle at the base.

Square pyramid: base 12 cm, height 8 cm. Half-base = 6. tan θ = 8/6 → θ = tan⁻¹(8/6) = 53.1°. Slant height = √(64 + 36) = 10 cm.
5

Always draw the extracted triangle separately — label every known side before writing any formula

This is the most important habit in 3D trig. After identifying the triangle, draw it completely separately from the 3D diagram — a clean, simple right-angled triangle with no clutter from the 3D picture. Label all three sides with their values or unknowns. Mark the right angle with a small square. Mark the angle you want with θ. Only then write the trig formula or Pythagoras equation.

Once the extracted triangle is drawn and labelled, choose your tool: if you have two sides and want the third, use Pythagoras. If you have one side and want an angle, or have an angle and want a side, use SOH CAH TOA. Identify which sides are opposite, adjacent, and hypotenuse relative to the angle θ you marked. Then write the ratio and solve.

Extracted triangle: opposite = 5 (height), adjacent = 10 (face diagonal), angle θ at bottom corner. Use tan θ = opp/adj = 5/10 = 0.5. θ = tan⁻¹(0.5) = 26.6°.
6

In the event of the angle between a line and a plane — use the projected line on the plane as the adjacent side

Exam questions sometimes ask for "the angle that line AG makes with the plane ABCD." The plane ABCD is the base of the cuboid. The angle between a line and a plane is the angle between the line and its projection onto the plane. The projection of the space diagonal AG onto the base plane is the face diagonal AC — the shadow the space diagonal would cast straight downward onto the base. The angle you want is at vertex A, between AG and AC.

The right angle in this situation is always at C — the foot of the vertical. The triangle ACG has a right angle at C, with AC as the adjacent side (the horizontal projection on the base), CG as the opposite side (the height of the cuboid), and AG as the hypotenuse (the space diagonal). Use tan θ = CG / AC to find the angle of elevation from A to G above the base.

Cuboid 8×6×5. AC (face diagonal) = √(64+36) = 10. Angle AG with base: tan θ = 5/10 = 0.5 → θ = 26.6°. This is the angle between the space diagonal and the base plane.
