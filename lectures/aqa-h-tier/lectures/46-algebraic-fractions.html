Algebraic Fractions

Simplifying algebraic fractions by factorising and cancelling common factors

What This Is

An algebraic fraction is a fraction where the numerator, the denominator, or both contain algebraic expressions — for example, (x² + 3x) ÷ (x² + x − 6). Simplifying one means factorising the top and bottom separately, then cancelling any factor that appears in both. The process is exactly the same as simplifying a numerical fraction like 12/18 — you find what the top and bottom share, and you divide it out. The algebra makes it look harder than it is, but the principle is identical.

Key Terms You Must Know

Algebraic Fraction

A fraction where the numerator or denominator (or both) contains letters as well as numbers — for example, x/(x + 2) or (x² − 4)/(x + 2).

Factorise

Rewrite an expression as a product of its factors. For example, x² + 3x factorises to x(x + 3). You must do this before you can cancel anything.

Common Factor

A factor that divides exactly into both the numerator and the denominator. This is the term you are allowed to cancel — nothing else.

Cancel

Divide both the numerator and the denominator by the same factor, leaving a simpler fraction. You can only cancel a factor — a whole bracket — never part of a sum.

Simplified Form

The fraction after all common factors have been cancelled. No further cancellation is possible. This is the answer the mark scheme expects.

The Method — Step by Step
Grade 7–8 topic. Algebraic fractions appear on Edexcel Higher papers at 3–4 marks. Factorisation of quadratics is required knowledge before this topic. Every step below must be shown in full — the mark scheme awards separate marks for factorising the top and factorising the bottom.
1

Factorise the numerator — the top of the fraction

Look at the top of the fraction on its own and factorise it completely. For x² + 3x, every term contains x, so you factor out x to get x(x + 3). Treat the numerator as a standalone expression — ignore the denominator entirely at this point.

Write the factorised form of the numerator beside your working. x² + 3x becomes x(x + 3) because x × x = x² and x × 3 = 3x. Check by expanding: x(x + 3) = x² + 3x ✓. This earns the first method mark.

Numerator: x² + 3x = x(x + 3). Write this result down — it goes on top of the fraction.
2

Factorise the denominator — the bottom of the fraction

Now look at the bottom of the fraction on its own. For x² + x − 6, you need two brackets that multiply to give it. Find two numbers that multiply to −6 and add to +1 (the coefficient of x). Those numbers are +3 and −2, because 3 × (−2) = −6 and 3 + (−2) = +1.

Write the factorised form: x² + x − 6 = (x + 3)(x − 2). Check by expanding: (x + 3)(x − 2) = x² − 2x + 3x − 6 = x² + x − 6 ✓. Always expand and check — one sign error at this step costs you two marks.

Denominator: x² + x − 6 = (x + 3)(x − 2). Check by expanding before moving on.
3

Rewrite the whole fraction using both factorised forms

Replace the original numerator with its factorised form, and replace the original denominator with its factorised form. Write this as a single fraction. You now have x(x + 3) on top and (x + 3)(x − 2) on the bottom. This step is essential — it makes the common factor visible on the page so you can see what to cancel.

Scan the numerator and denominator for any factor that appears in both. Look at each bracket individually. The bracket (x + 3) appears once in the numerator and once in the denominator — this is your common factor. That is the bracket you will cancel.

Rewritten fraction: x(x + 3) over (x + 3)(x − 2). The common factor (x + 3) is now visible in both top and bottom.
4

Cancel the common factor and write the simplified answer

Draw a line through (x + 3) in the numerator and draw a line through (x + 3) in the denominator. You are dividing both top and bottom by the same factor — the same operation you perform when simplifying 6/9 by dividing both by 3. What remains in the numerator is x. What remains in the denominator is (x − 2).

Write the simplified fraction using what remains: x on top and (x − 2) on the bottom. This is the fully simplified answer: x / (x − 2). No further cancellation is possible because x and (x − 2) share no common factor. This is the A1 mark.

After cancelling (x + 3) from top and bottom: answer is x / (x − 2).
5

In the event of adding or subtracting algebraic fractions — find a common denominator first

If the question asks you to add or subtract two algebraic fractions — for example, 3/(x + 2) + 2/(x − 1) — you need a common denominator before you can combine them. Multiply the two denominators together: (x + 2)(x − 1) becomes the common denominator for both fractions.

Rewrite each fraction over the common denominator by multiplying each numerator by what the denominator is missing. The first fraction needs × (x − 1) top and bottom; the second needs × (x + 2) top and bottom. Then combine the numerators over the single common denominator and simplify.

3/(x+2) + 2/(x−1) = [3(x−1) + 2(x+2)] / [(x+2)(x−1)] = (5x + 1) / [(x+2)(x−1)].

Cancelling terms in a sum is the single most penalised error on algebraic fractions!

0 / 3 marks
You cannot cancel x² out of x² + 3x against x² in the denominator. You can only cancel a factor that divides the entire expression — not a term added onto something else. In 12 + 6, you cannot cancel the 6 against the 6 in 6 + 3 because the 6 is part of a sum, not a factor of the whole thing. The same rule applies in algebra. Factorise first: x² + 3x = x(x + 3). Now x is a factor of the whole numerator. Only then can you look for something to cancel between top and bottom.

✓ Factorise denominator: x² + x − 6 = (x + 3)(x − 2)
