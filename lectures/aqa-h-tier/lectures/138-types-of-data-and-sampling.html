Types of Data and Sampling

Classify what type of data you have, then use the stratified sampling formula to pick the right number from each group.

What This Is

Data questions ask you to do two things: classify what kind of data is being collected, and calculate how many people to include from each group when taking a sample. Classifying data means deciding whether it can be described in words or numbers, and whether those numbers can only take set values or can be measured to any level of precision. Sampling means selecting a smaller group from a larger population — stratified sampling ensures the proportions in your sample match the proportions in the whole population. This topic appears at the start of statistics questions and as a standalone 2–4 mark question on every paper.

Key Terms You Must Know

Qualitative Data

Data described in words or categories, not numbers. Favourite colour, type of car, and opinion responses are all qualitative. You cannot calculate a mean from qualitative data.

Quantitative Data

Data described using numbers. It splits into two types: discrete (counted values that can only be whole numbers, like shoe size or number of siblings) and continuous (measured values that can take any value in a range, like height or weight).

Discrete Data

Quantitative data that can only take specific, separate values with no values possible between them. Number of goals scored, number of students in a class. You cannot score 2.7 goals.

Continuous Data

Quantitative data that can take any value within a range — limited only by the precision of the measuring instrument. Height, temperature, time, and mass are all continuous.

Stratified Sample

A sample where each group (stratum) in the population is represented in the same proportion as it appears in the full population. The formula is: (group size ÷ total population) × sample size.

The Method — Step by Step
Grade 4–7 topic. Classifying data earns Grade 4–5 marks. Stratified sampling calculations push into Grade 6–7.
1

Ask: can this data be described in words or only in numbers?

Look at what is being measured or recorded. Ask yourself: is the answer always a number, or could it be a word or category? If the answer is always a word or label — a colour, a type, a yes/no — the data is qualitative.

If the answer is always a number, the data is quantitative. Numbers that describe quantities — height, score, time, temperature — are all quantitative. Move to Step 2 to classify further.

Example: "What is your favourite subject?" → answers are words (Maths, English, Science) → qualitative. "How tall are you?" → answer is a number → quantitative.
2

For quantitative data: ask whether it is counted or measured

Ask: is this data counted or measured? Counted data can only be whole numbers — you cannot have 2.5 children or 3.7 goals. Data that is counted is discrete.

Data that is measured — height, weight, time, temperature — can take any value and is only limited by how precisely you measure it. This data is continuous. Ask yourself: could a value fall between two whole numbers? If yes, it is continuous.

Example: Number of goals in a match → counted, only whole numbers → discrete. Time taken to run 100 m → measured, can be 12.47 s → continuous.
3

Identify the population, the sample, and the group sizes from the question

Read the question and extract three numbers: the total population size, each group's size, and the total sample size required. Write them down before any calculation — these are the only numbers that go into the stratified sampling formula.

Check that the group sizes add up to the total population. Year 10 (80) + Year 11 (160) = 240. This is a mandatory check — if they do not add up, you have misread the question.

Example: "A school has 240 students: 80 in Year 10 and 160 in Year 11. A stratified sample of 30 is taken." Population = 240, groups = 80 and 160, sample = 30.
4

Apply the stratified sampling formula: (group size ÷ total population) × sample size

Write the formula first. The formula always has the same structure: write the group size on top, the total population on the bottom, and multiply by the required sample size. Do this separately for each group the question asks about.

Substitute the values for the group you need. For Year 11: write 160 on top, 240 on the bottom, multiply by 30. The fraction 160/240 simplifies to 2/3. Then 2/3 × 30 = 20.

Always check your answer is a whole number — you cannot include 10.3 people in a sample. If the answer is not a whole number, round to the nearest whole. Then check that all group samples add up to the total required sample size.

Example: Year 11 sample = (160 ÷ 240) × 30 = (2/3) × 30 = 20. Year 10 sample = (80 ÷ 240) × 30 = 10. Check: 10 + 20 = 30 ✓
5

In the event of a question with three or more strata — apply the formula once for each group

If the population is split into three or more groups — for example Year 9, Year 10, and Year 11 — apply the exact same formula separately for every group the question asks about. The denominator is always the total population, never a sub-group.

Apply the formula to Year 9: (60 ÷ 300) × 30 = 6. Year 10: (80 ÷ 300) × 30 = 8. Year 11: (160 ÷ 300) × 30 = 16. Check: 6 + 8 + 16 = 30 ✓. The denominator is always 300, the total population.

In the event of three strata: apply (group ÷ total) × sample size for every group. The total population in the denominator never changes. Check all group samples add to the required total.
