Circle Theorems

Finding angles using the rules that every circle obeys — AQA

What This Is

Circle theorems are rules about angles that are always true inside and around a circle. Every time you see an angle formed by a chord, a tangent, or an arc, one of these rules tells you its exact size — you do not need to measure it. You use these rules to find missing angles by writing down which theorem applies and showing your reasoning, exactly as the mark scheme expects.

Key Terms You Must Know

Chord

A straight line joining any two points on the circumference. It does not have to pass through the centre. The diameter is the longest possible chord.

Arc

Part of the circumference. A minor arc is the shorter part between two points; a major arc is the longer part. An arc subtends the angles that sit on it.

Subtend

An angle is subtended by an arc or chord when the two arms of the angle reach out to the endpoints of that arc or chord. This word appears in every circle theorem question.

Tangent

A straight line that touches the circle at exactly one point and does not cross it. At the point of contact, the tangent is always perpendicular to the radius.

Cyclic quadrilateral

A four-sided shape whose four corners all sit on the circumference of the same circle. Opposite angles in a cyclic quadrilateral always add up to 180°.

The Method — Step by Step
Grade 6–9 topic. Circle theorems appear on every AQA exam paper at this grade. The key skill is identifying which theorem applies and stating it clearly in your working — the theorem name earns a method mark even when your arithmetic is right.
1

Identify the centre, circumference points, and any special lines

Look at the diagram and mark every point that sits on the circumference with a mental note — these are the endpoints of arcs and chords that the theorems describe. The centre, if shown, is usually labelled O.

Check whether any line is a tangent (touches once), a radius (centre to circumference), or a diameter (passes through centre). Spotting these shapes determines which theorem you need.

2

Match the angle to the correct circle theorem

Look at where the angle sits. An angle at the centre is formed by two radii. An angle at the circumference is formed at a point on the circle. If both are standing on the same arc, you need the angle at the centre theorem.

Check whether the four vertices of a quadrilateral all sit on the circumference. If they do, you have a cyclic quadrilateral and opposite angles add to 180°. This is a different theorem — do not confuse it with the angles-in-same-segment theorem.

3

Write the theorem name and apply the rule to find the angle

Write the theorem name as your reason — the mark scheme awards the method mark for this statement alone. Write it as: "Angle at the centre = 2 × angle at the circumference" or "Opposite angles in a cyclic quadrilateral add to 180°."

Apply the rule using the number given. If the angle at the centre is 86°, divide by 2 to find the angle at the circumference: 86 ÷ 2 = 43°. If you are given the circumference angle, multiply by 2 to find the centre angle.

4

Use the angle-in-a-semicircle theorem when the chord is a diameter

Spot a diameter — a chord that passes through centre O. Any angle at the circumference that stands on a diameter is always 90°. This is the angle-in-a-semicircle theorem and it is one of the most frequently tested rules.

Write your working: "AB is a diameter, so angle APB = 90° (angle in a semicircle)." Then use the remaining angles in triangle APB — they must add to 180° — to find any other unknown angle in the triangle.

5

Apply the tangent–radius theorem when a tangent appears

Spot a tangent line — it meets the circle at one point only. Draw or recognise the radius to that exact point of contact. The angle between the tangent and the radius is always 90°. Write: "tangent and radius meet at 90°."

Use the 90° angle as a known angle inside any triangle that the diagram creates. With the tangent and radius together forming a right angle, apply angles-in-a-triangle to find the remaining unknown.

6

Apply the angles-in-same-segment theorem for two angles on one arc

Look for two separate angles both touching the same arc from the same side of the chord. Both angles are subtended by the same arc and both sit in the same segment. These two angles are always equal — write: "angles in the same segment are equal."

7

In the event of two theorems needed in one question — chain the reasons

Spot when a question gives one angle and asks for another angle that is not directly linked by a single theorem. You must find an intermediate angle first, then apply a second theorem to reach the answer. Write each step with its theorem name — skipping the intermediate step loses the method mark.

Write each reason on a separate line: "Step 1: angle at centre = 2 × angle at circumference → centre angle = 2 × 38° = 76°. Step 2: opposite angles in cyclic quadrilateral add to 180° → missing angle = 180° − 76° = 104°." Both reasons must appear for full marks.
